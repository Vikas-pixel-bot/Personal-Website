Bridging the Upper-Primary Learning Cliff: Notes on the 48-Day FLN Catch-Up Course (Grades 5–7)

FLN TaRL & LSRW Grades 5–7 500 Schools 6 min read

In national education policy, Foundational Literacy and Numeracy (FLN) is almost always framed as an early-primary goal for Grades 1 through 3. Yet when you walk into a residential tribal ashram school in Nashik, Thane, Nagpur, or Amravati, you encounter a much quieter crisis in Grades 5, 6, and 7: older students who are expected to analyze science chapters and solve ratio problems while still struggling to decode connected paragraphs or subtract two-digit numbers with regrouping.

Left unaddressed, this creates an upper-primary learning cliff. Once a student enters Grade 6 without foundational fluency, every subject textbook becomes a wall of unreadable symbols. To address this across 500 Ashramshalas under the Majhi Shala, Majha Swabhimaan partnership with the Tribal Development Department, we designed and rolled out a structured 48-day FLN Catch-Up Course built specifically for Grades 5–7.

Why Upper-Primary Remediation Demands Dignity

The biggest mistake in remedial education is handing a twelve-year-old a Grade 1 primer. Older learners know they are behind; infantilizing materials only deepen disengagement and classroom shame. A catch-up course for Grades 5–7 must bridge Grade 1–3 foundational competencies while respecting the social maturity of an upper-primary student.

Instead of nursery rhymes and isolated letter drills, we grounded our activities in contexts familiar to residential students: weekly rural markets, sports scoreboards, hostel rations, agricultural measurements, and local folktales.

The Dual Engine: LSRW for Language, TaRL for Numeracy

The 48-day curriculum runs on two complementary pedagogical tracks within a dedicated 90-minute daily window:

  • Language (LSRW Progression): Rather than jumping straight to textbook reading, each cycle moves sequentially through Listening, Speaking, Reading, and Writing. Oral fluency and structured pair dialogue build confidence before students decode leveled text cards and compose short, expressive sentences of their own.
  • Mathematics (Teaching at the Right Level, TaRL): Students are grouped strictly by demonstrated mastery rather than age or grade, progressing from 1-digit recognition and place value to 2-digit operations, borrowing/regrouping, and real-world word problems. Every 12 days, rapid diagnostic checkpoints allow students to move up groups as soon as they master a milestone.

Views on Implementation: What Actually Happens on the Ground

Designing a 48-day syllabus on paper takes weeks; getting 500 residential schools to execute it consistently is a different discipline altogether. Three field realities shaped how we implemented the course:

1. Negotiating the 90-Minute Timetable Window

Ashramshalas run on tightly packed residential schedules: morning assembly, breakfast, regular periods, sports, and evening study hours. If teachers view a catch-up course as "extra syllabus" that threatens textbook completion, it gets quietly dropped within two weeks. Instead of imposing a mandate from above, we sat with Headmasters and Hostel Superintendents, walked them through their school's baseline gaps, and demonstrated that covering a Grade 6 textbook with non-readers yields zero exam retention. Formalizing a protected 90-minute daily block during regular hours required administrative empathy, not just circulars.

2. Bridging Home Dialects to Standard Classroom Text

Many first-generation learners in Maharashtra's tribal belts grow up speaking Pawari, Bhili, Korku, or Gondi at home, while school textbooks and state assessments are in standard Marathi or Hindi. When instructors silence a child's home language, classroom participation drops to zero. During the first 16 days of the Catch-Up course, we instituted a deliberate bilingual bridge: facilitators encouraged students to explain mathematical logic and story ideas in their mother tongue first, before mapping those exact concepts onto standard Marathi or Hindi words.

3. Classroom Co-Teaching Over Lecture Workshops

One-day cascade training workshops rarely change what happens behind a closed classroom door. Managing three distinct TaRL learning groups simultaneously in one room looks intimidating on paper. Rather than lecturing teachers about pedagogy, our 60 field coordinators spent 80% of their time co-teaching inside classrooms, physically demonstrating how to transition groups, use tactile number strips, and run quick 2-minute formative checks without classroom chaos.
