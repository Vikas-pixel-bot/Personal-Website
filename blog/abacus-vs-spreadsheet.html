Why a Wooden Abacus Still Beats a Spreadsheet in the Classroom

TLM Pedagogy Activity-Based Learning February 2026 5 min read

I've sat through a lot of education technology pitches. Tablets with pre-loaded content. Adaptive learning apps. AI tutors that personalise the experience. The promise is always the same: technology will solve the learning problem that teachers and textbooks couldn't.

And then I visit a classroom in Amravati or Nashik, and I watch a Class 2 student pick up an abacus for the first time — sliding beads, counting out loud, correcting herself, laughing when she gets it right — and I think: this is what learning looks like. Not a screen. A wooden frame, some coloured beads, and a child whose hands are doing the thinking.

A Classroom in Jaamganvahan, Kalamunuri

During a field visit in February, I watched a Shiksha Mitra run a numeracy session using nothing but an abacus and a set of number cards. The children were Class 1 and 2 — most of them boarding students at the ashramshala, many from first-generation learner families.

The session had no PowerPoint. No projector. No app. What it had was structure, pace, and a teacher who understood exactly what she was trying to accomplish: she wanted children to understand place value — the idea that the position of a digit matters, not just its shape. The abacus made that idea concrete. You could see the tens and the units. You could move them. You could make mistakes and fix them without any stigma.

By the end of 30 minutes, children who had previously struggled to count reliably past 20 were confidently working with two-digit numbers. Not because the abacus is magic. Because the teacher had a clear learning objective, the right tool for that objective, and the skill to use it well.

What TLM Actually Does

Teaching Learning Materials — TLMs — are physical objects designed to make abstract concepts tangible. An abacus for place value. 3D letter tiles for phonics. Picture cards for vocabulary. A number line painted on the floor for ordering and comparison. They work because learning is not primarily a cognitive process. It is an embodied one. Children learn through their hands, their movement, their senses. Abstract symbols on a blackboard are the last step in understanding, not the first.

The TLM kits we distribute across ashram schools under the Mazi Shala, Maza Swabhiman program are built around this principle. Each kit is grade-appropriate, curriculum-aligned, and designed to be used without electricity or internet. This matters enormously in the schools we work with, where connectivity is unreliable and power cuts are common.

Why Ed-Tech Misses This

I am not opposed to technology in education. What I'm opposed to is the assumption that technology is the upgrade — that going from a wooden abacus to a touchscreen app is inherently an improvement. It often isn't. Here's why.

Technology mediates the learning. The child interacts with a screen, not with the concept. The feedback is automated, which means the teacher doesn't have to understand what the child misunderstood — the app just tells the child to try again. The relationship between teacher, student, and knowledge gets bypassed by the interface.

A good TLM doesn't do that. It puts the concept in the child's hands and forces the teacher to observe closely. What does the child do when they're stuck? Where do they hesitate? What misconception does the error reveal? These are the questions that make a teacher better. A well-designed physical material creates the conditions for a teacher to ask and answer them.

What Teachers Need to Sustain It

The honest challenge with TLMs isn't the materials. It's the usage. In the schools where our kits gathered dust on a shelf — and there were some — the problem was never that teachers didn't care. It was that they didn't know specifically how to integrate the materials into a structured lesson. A kit without a lesson plan is just a box of things.

This is why our teacher training program spends significant time on activity design: not just "here is an abacus" but "here is how you use this abacus to teach addition to a Class 2 student who already understands number recognition but gets confused by carrying." Context, sequence, and purpose. The same material can be powerful or useless depending on whether the teacher understands its specific function.

The Policy Conversation We're Not Having

Every large education reform conversation I've been part of eventually circles back to technology as the solution. More devices. Better connectivity. Digital content. These are real investments and they have a place. But there is almost no policy conversation about TLM procurement, teacher training in material-based pedagogy, or maintaining and refreshing physical learning aids.

The abacus doesn't have a lobby. The ed-tech company does.

I'm not making a luddite argument. I'm making an evidence argument. In the classrooms I've visited across Nashik, Nagpur, Amravati, and Thane, the best learning moments happened when a child had something in their hands, a teacher who knew what to do with it, and no internet required. That combination shouldn't be treated as a consolation prize for under-resourced schools. It should be treated as a model worth learning from.
