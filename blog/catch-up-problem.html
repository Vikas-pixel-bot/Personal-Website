The Catch-Up Problem: Reaching Children Who Fell Behind Before We Arrived

Catch-Up Remedial Education TaRL January 2026 6 min read

There is a student in a Class 6 classroom at a government ashramshala in Nagpur who cannot read a paragraph. Not because she lacks intelligence. Not because her teachers failed to show up. But because somewhere between Class 1 and now, the system moved her forward without checking whether she was ready — and nobody designed a response for what happens when children accumulate years of unaddressed learning gaps.

She is not an exception. She is representative. Across the 500+ tribal residential schools that the Mazi Shala, Maza Swabhiman program works in, the same pattern repeats: upper primary students — Classes 5, 6, and 7 — sitting through age-appropriate syllabuses while carrying foundational deficits they never got the chance to close. The curriculum expects them to analyse text. They're still working out how to decode it.

The Class 6 Student Who Couldn't Read

I keep coming back to this image because it clarifies what "remedial education" actually means in practice. It's not about students who are less capable. It's about students whose learning trajectory got disrupted — by irregular schooling, by language barriers, by the absence of foundational support at the critical early stages — and who arrived in upper primary carrying a debt they didn't know how to name.

When our baseline assessments first captured this reality in detail, the numbers were difficult to share. Not because they were surprising to people who work in these schools, but because acknowledging them honestly requires also acknowledging that the system created them. That's a harder conversation than talking about innovation.

What TaRL Actually Means

Teaching at the Right Level is a deceptively simple idea: assess where a child actually is, group them by learning level rather than age or grade, and teach to that level until they move up. It was developed by Pratham and has been tested across India and several other countries with consistent results: when you meet children where they are, they learn faster than when you teach to where the syllabus says they should be.

The philosophical shift sounds obvious. The implementation is genuinely hard. It requires teachers to temporarily set aside the grade-level curriculum — which is what they're evaluated on — and spend time on foundational skills that the system considers "already done." It requires administrators to tolerate a visible divergence between what's being taught and what the textbook says. It requires a program structure that can hold this space without causing chaos in the school timetable.

Our Catch-Up Course for Classes 5–7 is built on TaRL principles. It's interactive, activity-based, and structured into daily sessions that can fit within the existing school schedule without displacing the regular curriculum entirely. The materials — fill-in activities, visual aids, audio-visual components — are designed to be usable by teachers who haven't had extensive TaRL training, while still being effective.

Rolling It Out Across 500 Schools

In practice, rolling out a new instructional approach across 500+ schools in four divisions is a coordination challenge as much as a pedagogical one. Every school has a different superintendent. Every classroom has a different teacher. Every division has a different project officer with different relationships with the schools under their jurisdiction.

What I've learned is that the first conversation is the most important one. Not the training — the conversation before the training, where you establish with a principal or superintendent that the program understands the school's constraints and isn't asking teachers to do the impossible. Trust precedes adoption. In schools where the Shiksha Mitra had built a genuine relationship with the staff — where the visit was welcomed rather than tolerated — implementation was stronger, more consistent, and more creative.

In schools where the Catch-Up materials were handed over without that relational foundation, the kits sat unused or were used mechanically, without the spirit of the approach.

What the Intensive Pilot Taught Us

Alongside the standard Catch-Up rollout, we ran an Intensive Catch-Up pilot in 36 selected schools — a more closely mentored implementation model where Shiksha Mitras were more present, teacher support was more hands-on, and feedback loops were tighter. The idea was to test whether depth of support, rather than breadth of coverage, produced better outcomes.

Early observations suggest yes. The 36 schools showed faster movement in learning levels, more consistent daily implementation, and — interestingly — more teacher buy-in, because teachers felt supported rather than monitored. The pilot is still underway and the endline data will tell us whether the gains are significant. But the relational dynamic it created is already informing how we think about the non-intensive rollout.

What "Remedial" Should Actually Mean

The word "remedial" carries a stigma it doesn't deserve. In the development sector's language, it's often used apologetically — as if working on foundational skills with upper primary students is an admission of failure. I think we should reclaim the word and be proud of what it represents.

Remedial education means taking seriously the child in front of you, rather than the grade level on their enrollment form. It means designing a response to the actual situation, rather than the idealized one. It means believing that a Class 6 student who can't read a paragraph today can read one six months from now — and building the program structure that makes that possible.

The student I mentioned at the beginning of this post — the one in Nagpur who couldn't read a paragraph in Class 6 — I don't know if she's in one of our program schools. But I know that somewhere in this system, there are thousands of students like her. The question the Catch-Up Course is trying to answer isn't "why did this happen?" It's: "given that it has happened, what do we do now?"

That's the question that matters. And the answer is always the same: meet them where they are, and move from there.
