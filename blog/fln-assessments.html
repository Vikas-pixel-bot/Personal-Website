What 8,575 Assessments Taught Me About Learning Gaps in Tribal Schools

FLN Assessment Tribal Education March 2026 7 min read

Numbers are easy to produce in the development sector. The harder thing is to be honest about what they mean. When our team completed midline assessments across ashram schools in Maharashtra — 8,575 students in Classes 1 through 4 — the numbers told a story that was both encouraging and humbling.

Reading proficiency at the Story level had jumped from 14.1% to 35%. That's a real gain. Children who a few months ago couldn't string letters into words were now reading simple paragraphs. But the same data showed that nearly a third of students were still at the Letter or Beginner level — unable to reliably identify individual letters. These are children in Class 3 and 4. The system has been moving them forward for years without checking whether the foundation was solid.

The Baseline Numbers Nobody Wants to Talk About

When we ran our initial baseline assessment at the start of the program, the results were stark. In numeracy, over 40% of students in Classes 1–4 were at the Beginner level — unable to recognise numbers 1 to 9 reliably. In reading, more than a quarter couldn't identify basic letters. This wasn't a surprise to the field teams who work in these schools daily. It was a surprise, I suspect, to some of the stakeholders who had been reading annual progress reports framed around curriculum completion.

Curriculum completion is the wrong metric. It measures what was taught, not what was learned. An ashramshala teacher who gets through the prescribed syllabus by March has done their job on paper — but if their Class 3 students can't read a sentence independently, something has failed somewhere between the textbook and the child.

What the Midline Revealed

The midline assessment, completed in February 2026, gave us our first real comparison point. The gains were real: beginner-level readers dropped significantly across the board, and story-level readers nearly tripled. In numeracy, more students were confidently working with subtraction and division — skills that required genuine understanding, not just rote counting.

But the gains were uneven. Schools where Shiksha Mitras had been consistent — showing up, using TLM kits, running structured activities — showed the sharpest improvements. Schools with attendance gaps, or where the Catch-Up materials hadn't been distributed effectively, showed smaller movement. The data made visible what field visits had suggested: implementation consistency is the actual variable that determines outcomes.

Why the Gap Exists

The learning gap in tribal residential schools isn't primarily a teacher problem or a curriculum problem. It's a systems problem. Children in ashramshalas often arrive from remote communities with limited prior exposure to formal learning environments. Some are first-generation learners. Many speak home languages that differ from the medium of instruction. And the schools themselves — residential, often under-resourced, managed by superintendents who carry enormous administrative loads — are not always structured to catch children who fall behind early.

Once a child falls behind in Class 1, the gap tends to widen. The curriculum moves on. The teacher has 40 other students. By Class 5, a child who never solidified number recognition is sitting through lessons on fractions. By Class 8, they're disengaged. The system records a dropout. What actually happened started years earlier.

What Honest Assessment Design Looks Like

The TaRL (Teaching at the Right Level) assessment framework we use doesn't ask whether a child has covered Chapter 5. It asks: can this child read a word? A sentence? A paragraph? A story? Can they recognise numbers? Do basic addition? Subtract? The levels are simple and observable. A trained Shiksha Mitra can conduct the assessment in under 10 minutes per student, and the result is a genuine picture of where that child actually is.

This kind of assessment is uncomfortable for institutions because it reveals how much the official grade level diverges from actual learning levels. It also creates accountability. If you know that 28% of your Class 3 students can't read a word, you can't unknow it. You have to respond to it — or explain why you didn't.

What Changed in Our Approach

After the baseline, we made two significant changes. First, we introduced spot assessments alongside the formal midline — smaller, informal checks conducted during school visits to verify that assessment data was accurate and that the classroom reality matched the numbers. Second, we shifted the framing in teacher training away from "covering content" toward "checking comprehension." Simple in theory. Genuinely difficult to implement in a system where teachers have been evaluated on curriculum completion for their entire careers.

The endline assessment in a few months will tell us whether the gains held and whether the most behind students made the most progress — which is the real test of a catch-up intervention. But the more lasting outcome, I hope, is that 500+ schools now have a culture of asking a different question: not "have we taught this?" but "have they learned it?"

That shift is harder to measure. But it's the one that matters most.
